10   In an analysis of over one million ChatGPT conversations, practical guidance, in-
11   formation seeking, and collaborative writing cover approximately 75% of all user
12   interactions (Chatterji et al., 2025). Furthermore, a substantial portion of collab-
13   orative writing interactions directly pertain to political topics (Zhao et al., 2024).
14   Given the order of magnitude LLMs operate on, a small fraction still means millions
15   of people, like Terrell.

16   When such a tool sits inside the drafting process of millions of writers, its defaults
17   and behavioral tendencies start to matter. Communication literature terms this
18   AI-mediated communication: when an AI system modifies or generates text on a
19   person’s behalf, the system’s properties become properties of the message [Hancock et
20   al. 2020]. And those properties aggregate. Writers using generative models produce
21   measurably more homogeneous text, converging on the framings the model favours
22   [cite Doshi & Hauser 2024; Padmakumar & He 2024]. Epstein & Robertson [cite
23   2015] shows that when a single system’s dispositions touch enough people, they move
24   aggregate attitudes. This suggests a pathway for AI as input to public discourse.

25   To add, AI writing assistance demonstrably moves human opinion. Jakesch et al.
26   (2023) show experimentally that co-writing with an opinionated language model
27   produces latent persuasion: the assistant makes it frictionless to express certain
28   views and quietly e!ortful to express others, and the writer’s own opinion drifts
29   toward the assistant’s. Participants writing with a skewed assistant were twice as
30   likely to argue the assistant’s side as participants writing alone, and their privately
31   reported attitudes moved to match.

32   Williams-Ceci et al. (2026) find that biased autocomplete suggestions shift attitudes
33   on societal issues while participants remain unaware that any influence occurred, and
34   Fisher et al. (2025) find the same for politically biased chat models in decision-making
35   tasks. Furthermore, light human oversight does not erase the influence out of the text
36   itself: even when writers edit and approve AI-assisted drafts, the result systematically
37   misrepresents them to readers, appearing more opinionated than their unassisted

38   writing. Writers also prefer the AI-assisted text even after the distortion is pointed
39   out to them (Röttger et al., 2026). In interacting with an LLM in a seemingly
40   benign way, Terrell’s political expression, and his political opinions, are being quietly
41   co-shaped by the AI.

42   Early audits of LLM political behaviour focused primarily on the risk of LLMs
43   converging on a single political stance dictated either by their pre-training data or
44   post training reinforcement learning (RLHF). The commercial trajectory of LLMs
45   has since created a new, arguably more pressing threat model. Personalisation is
46   now a default, persistent feature across leading AI services, implemented by injecting
47   user profiles and histories directly into system prompts (OpenAI, 2025; Anthropic,
48   2025; Google, 2025). The model no longer answers a question, it answers Terrell’s
49   question, conditioned on everything it has retained about who Terrell appears to be.
50   Kirk et al. (2024) argue that personalised alignment promises usefulness and softens
51   the one-size-fits-all cultural hegemony that has plagued traditional AI alignment
52   work, but also has the potential to enable large-scale profiling, reinforce biases, and
53   create individualized echo chambers.

54   Applied to political writing assistance, these risks compound. A uniformly biased
55   assistant at least exposes every user to the same skew, which is visible, auditable,
56   and correctable [cite]. A personalised assistant that tailors the political stance of
57   its writing to each inferred user reproduces the logic of the recommender-system
58   filter bubble (Kirk et al., 2024), except that instead of a feed curating what a person
59   reads, a writing assistant co-authors what a person says.

60   Combined with latent persuasion, the mechanism is clear: the model infers a world-
61   view from Terrell’s personal profile, writes to it, and the act of writing entrenches
62   that worldview to Terrell (Jakesch et al., 2023). This isn’t a hypothetical scenario
63   either. Field data from two weeks of real world usage shows that a condensed user
64   profile inferred by the model and held in memory is the single strongest driver of a it
65   mirroring the user’s political perspective (Jain et al., 2026) 1 . Furthermore, Soares
66   et al. (2026) find across 21 models that LLMs behave as “ideological chameleons”,
67   systematically shifting their judgements of political propositions to match a user’s
68   declared leaning.

 99   Nobody sits Terrell down and administers the Political Compass Test. He never
100   asks the model whether it “strongly agrees” that abortion should be legal; he asks
101   for help writing up his opinion. Yet the dominant convention in LLM political-bias
102   evaluation measures models exactly the way Terrell never encounters them: with
103   rigid multiple-choice questionnaires and fixed persona instructions.

104   The dominant convention fails to capture how bias actually manifests in the wild for
105   three reasons. First, multiple-choice testing lacks ecological validity, as previously
106   stated. Second, existing audits fail to account for implicit and passive memory
107   contexts, through which models react to inferred user characteristics/identity. Third,
108   audits that do vary user identity rarely anchor the observed shifts against real

109   opinion distributions, making it impossible to adjudicate whether a shift is calibrated
110   personalisation or stereotyped projection.

111   Even though recently, some papers have the open ended writing assistance [cite
112   them], no existing audit combines open-ended writing tasks, the recent memory
113   personalisation identity mechanism users actually experience, and a survey-anchored
114   benchmark that can tell personalisation from projection. That combination is the
115   gap this thesis fills.

116   In this thesis, we systematically vary the political and demographic cues stored
117   in an LLM’s memory while holding all other inputs constant. We then test a
118   diverse set of state-of-the-art language (SoTA) models on writing tasks designed
119   to elicit a position on a political issue or proposition. This design constitutes a
120   quantitative, counterfactual experimental audit in which user-identity cues serve as
121   the isolated independent variables. To adjudicate whether the models’ behavioural
122   shifts represent valid personalisation or stereotyping, we anchor the results against
123   the 2025 Cooperative Election Study (CES) Common Content. As such, this study
124   is entirely restricted to the socio-political context of the United States, utilizing
125   US-centric policy topics, partisan dynamics, and demographic baselines.

127   2        Background
128   2.1       History of AI political bias measurement
129   Political bias in LLMs was first studied as if it were a fixed property of the model;
130   bias as deviation from a neutral point on a left–right scale. In this setting, researchers
131   administer political questionnaires and ask whether a given model appears more
132   liberal or conservative2 (Buyl et al., 2026). These audits established that LLMs
133   are not politically neutral instruments, and lean more liberal at default across
134   instruments, languages, and model families. However, in real-world use, LLMs are
135   not normally encountered as survey respondents. They are encountered as interactive
136   systems embedded in writing, advice, and search workflows. A stance expressed
137   under forced-choice testing may not necessarily predict the stance expressed in open
138   generation.

139   A second wave, still using surveys as the primary instrument, benchmarked model
140   opinion against human opinion distributions. This human baseline makes it possible
141   to assess not only whether a model leans left or right, but also how well its responses
142   track the distribution and heterogeneity of real human opinions. Santurkar et al.
143   (2023) introduce OpinionQA, comparing LLM responses to Pew American Trends
144   Panel (ATP) data across US demographic groups. They find that model opinions
145   are misaligned with real group-level opinions, and models trained with human
146   feedback tend to express opinions closer to some demographic groups than others.
147   This misalignment persists even when models are explicitly prompted to answer as
148   members of a particular demographic group, with the models tending to collapse
149   diversity into a stereotyped response. Durmus et al. (2024) extend the approach
150   cross-nationally and show that opinion distributions can be steered by prompting,
151   but minority viewpoints remain systematically under-represented.

152   Röttger et al. (2025) move to a more ecologically valid setting that motivates this
153   thesis. Their primary contribution, IssueBench, constructs millions of realistic
154   prompts by crossing writing-assistance templates mined from real user logs with
155   contested political issues, and shows that issue-level bias in open-ended writing is
156   common, persistent, and similar across language models. Instead of treating models
157   as survey respondents, they tests models and document their bias under realistic
158   prompting conditions; the form most users actually encounter.

159   Röttger et al. (2025), however, audits the default user. Törnberg and Schimmel
160   (2026) demonstrate that this default user is itself an implicit persona. The natural
161   next question, therefore, is how these biases manifest when models are given cues
162   about who the user is.

163   2.2   Personalisation and memory
164   Personalisation is the mechanism through which user identity now reaches the model.
165   Kirk et al. (2024) define personalised alignment as models adapting to user-specific
166   data, and document how leading model providers now incorporate this feature
167   as a default. This is mediated by an on-by-default feature called memory. It is a
168   mechanism that allows LLMs to retain and recall information from past conversations
169   in order to produce more contextually coherent responses. In practice, memory can
170   be created either explicitly, when users ask the system to remember something, or
171   implicitly, when the system infers that information from ordinary conversation and
172   stores it without a direct request [cite OpenAI]. This means that the traditional
173   audit methodology not specifying the user is no longer measuring a model in its
174   current deployed configuration.

175   Kirk et al. (2024) also argue that the bounds of personalisation should be set
176   deliberately, documented, and open to contestation. They reason that despite
177   its potential benefits of enhancing usefulness and minimising cultural hegemony,
178   personalised alignment risks profiling at scale, manipulation, echo chambers, and bias
179   reinforcement. Thus, providing a standard against which the behaviours documented
180   in this thesis can be assessed.

181   On the theme of personalised alignment, Törnberg and Schimmel (2026) admin-
182   ister the Political Compass Test, the Pew Political Typology, and 1,540 partisan-
183   benchmarked Pew ATP items to six frontier models while varying only the stated
184   identity of the asker. They find that a Republican cue shifts responses conservative
185   roughly eight times as far as a Democrat cue shifts them liberal. Their asker, however,
186   is declared by the user in the current exchange and answers on forced-choice instru-
187   ments. Although sharing the same premise, this thesis deviates from their approach
188   to ask what happens when the identity is not explicitly declared in conversation but
189   stored in memory, and the task is not a questionnaire but writing assistance.

190   Jain et al. (2026) motivate this methodological deviation. They find that among all
191   tested drivers of political perspective sycophancy in multi-week real-world usage, a
192   condensed user profile injected into memory has a larger e!ect than opinions the
193   user states in conversation. Soares et al. (2026) report the same directionality across
194   21 models. Memory is therefore not one personalisation channel among several, but
195   the exact mechanism by which user identity most powerfully and most persistently
196   reaches deployed models.

197   Dash et al. (2026) characterise ChatGPT’s memory as a standing representation of the
198   user derived from conversational traces, which includes explicitly stated preferences,
199   as well as extracted personal data, contextual details, and psychological inferences.


                                                9
200   Empirically, they find that explicit political memories are rare (2%) relative to broad
201   personal information such as names, locations, and occupations (28%). So, if memory
202   a!ects political output at all, it may do so predominantly through material that is
203   not inherently political.

204   Mireshghallah et al. (2025) and Yoon et al. (2026) provide some empirical evidence,
205   finding indeed that stored user attributes in memory are leaked and reused across
206   task types inappropriately. This suggests the general behaviour that a memory entry,
207   once stored, is available to condition any subsequent output.

208   For political writing assistance, this raises a specific question. If a model stores
209   that a user lives in a particular state, studies at a particular university, works in a
210   particular occupation, or belongs to a particular social group, should that information
211   a!ect the political arguments it drafts? Some user context may be relevant to the
212   writing task. But relevance is not automatic, and a memory cue becomes politically
213   consequential when it is treated as a proxy for an unstated belief. If models do
214   mirror stated political beliefs, can/should/do they impute them from demographic
215   residue in memory?

216   2.3   Implicit personalisation and demographic inference
217   A growing body of work shows that models infer user identity from indirect cues and
218   then condition their behaviour on those inferences, a phenomenon Jin et al. (2024)
219   term implicit personalisation. The empirical evidence suggests this is particularly
220   persistent for first names. Salinas et al. (2025) audit 42 prompt templates and find that
221   GPT-4 systematically produces less favourable advice (in car-purchase negotiation,
222   election prediction, employment scenarios) when user names are associated with
223   racial minorities and women, with names associated with Black women receiving
224   the least advantageous responses from the model. Eloundou et al. (2025) find in a
225   name-based audit of ChatGPT tra"c that response quality, friendliness, and content
226   di!er systematically between White male names and other minority demographic
227   subgroups.

228   These together establish generally that a first name is su"cient to change a model’s
229   output. Whether a name does this specifically in the political context is a harder
230   question since a name carries no political information except potentially through
231   demographic association. For example, the name Aaliyah does not signal a stance on
232   abortion, aside from demographic information such as the race or gender typically
233   associated with the name.

234   With just explicit demographic information though, political stance does shift.
235   Santurkar et al. (2023) and Durmus et al. (2024) prompt models to respond to
236   political opinion surveys as members of particular demographic subgroups and find

                                                 10
237   that they do move, though they collapse within-group diversity into stereotyped
238   positions and under-represent minority viewpoints. Argyle et al. (2023) go further
239   and condition a model on sociodemographic backstories (party, race, gender, age,
240   ideology, and state of residence) drawn from ANES respondents and show that the
241   resulting response distributions can match those of real human subgroups. However,
242   in their construction of these backstories, they vary every attribute at once, which
243   makes the contribution of any single attribute unrecoverable.

244   Their design, therefore cannot adjudicate, for example, whether a state of residence
245   adds anything beyond the party identification sitting beside it in the same profile.
246   Furthermore, instructing a model to answer as a Black woman from Ohio produces a
247   performance of that identity, whereas storing that its user is a Black woman leaves
248   the model to decide, unprompted, what to do with the information. This thesis
249   deviates from traditional ‘persona prompting’ design of the former to test the latter.

250   Most of the literature measures demographic inference and its e!ects through changes
251   in model outputs. These behavioural evaluations, however, cannot distinguish
252   between a model that never inferred a demographic attribute and one that inferred
253   it but chose not to express that inference in its response. While this distinction is
254   primarily scientific, it is also increasingly relevant in current LLM systems since they
255   maintain persistent user models, where latent representations may influence future
256   interactions even when they are not expressed immediately in output.

257   Neplenbroek et al. (2025) address this limitation by examining the model’s internal
258   representation of the user rather than relying solely on generated text. Using
259   controlled synthetic conversations in which explicit demographic statements and
260   stereotypical cues are independently manipulated, they train linear probes to detect
261   internally encoded demographic attributes. They show that models reliably encode
262   demographics users explicitly state, while also inferring demographic attributes from
263   stereotypical cues alone.

264   Taken together, the literature establishes that LLMs infer demographic information
265   from indirect cues and that such inferences can influence generated outputs, gen-
266   erally. Less is known about how individual demographic attributes a!ect political
267   output when supplied as persistent user information rather than explicit personas,
268   or about the relationship between demographic inference and behavioural expression
269   in this setting. These questions motivate the empirical analyses in the following
270   chapters, complementing behavioural evaluation with analyses of the model’s internal
271   representations.

272   2.4   Measuring what is fair
273   When is a user memory-conditioned shift in model behaviour desirable? and how do
274   we distinguish stereotyping from appropriate personalisation? Kantharuban et al.
275   (2025) show that LLM recommendations change systematically in response to user
276   identity cues across product, content, and service domains. They ask the question this
277   thesis inherits: are such shifts legitimate personalisation, matching real preferences
278   of the user’s group, or stereotyping, projecting assumed ones? However, without a
279   ground-truth benchmark for real group preferences they are unable to tell whether
280   the observed shift is calibrated or stereotyped.

281   Wang et al. (2025b) provide the fairness framework for discussing this question
282   comprehensively. They argue that algorithmic fairness has defaulted to di!erence-
283   unaware treatment, that is, asking a model to behave identically across groups.
284   In some contexts, this is the wrong target, because the groups genuinely di!er in
285   ways that the model should be aware of. For example, di!erence-unawareness may
286   be desirable for a factual explanation or neutral information retrieval, but it may
287   constitute failure if a model writing for a Black user does not adapt the framing of
288   an essay on racial discrimination to reflect the lived experiences and perspectives of
289   Black Americans.

290   To adjudicate, they distinguish descriptive benchmarks (anchored in what is factually
291   true of groups) from normative benchmarks (anchored in what ought to be). Within
292   the context of this thesis, the distribution of political opinion across demographic and
293   partisan groups can be argued as descriptive quantity because it is an empirical fact,
294   measurable in survey data. For a descriptive domain, the di!erence-aware target then
295   becomes comparability to the real di!erence. In this setting, a model can fail in one
296   of two ways. It can miscalibrate the group mean (overshooting, undershooting, or
297   inverting the real di!erence), or collapse the group variance (treating a distribution
298   as a point).

299   Wang et al. (2025a) identify the latter with evidence from 3,200 human participants.
300   They conclude that current training pipelines leave models prone both to misportray-
301   ing group positions and to erasing the diversity within them. In fact, whether models
302   can meet the descriptive target at all is contested.

303   Argyle et al. (2023), discussed earlier, o!er the a"rmative case. Conditioned on
304   ANES backstories, GPT-3 produced subgroup response distributions comparable
305   to those of real respondents. Subsequent work has undercut its generality. ‘Silicon
306   samples’ have proven unstable across models, prompts, and time (Boelaert et al., 2025;
307   Bisbee et al., 2024). Additionally, sociodemographic prompting fails to reproduce
308   subjective judgements reliably (Sun et al., 2025).


                                                12
309   The a"rmative case therefore survives as an existence proof rather than a general
310   property. Furthermore, these outcomes have only been demonstrated through
311   instructed personas answering survey items, the first wave of bias auditing. Whether
312   calibration or its failure carries over to the memory and personalisation channels
313   shaping open-ended writing is untested, and is what this thesis measures.

314   On the point of calibration, bias in this thesis is therefore not distance from a
315   neutral midpoint, as the first wave of political audits construed it, but divergence
316   from the descriptive benchmark: stereotyping when a shift misrepresents a real
317   group di!erence, flattening when it erases one. A model that writes di!erently for
318   a Republican than for a Democrat is not thereby biased; a model that writes as
319   though Republicans are further conservative than they are, is.

320   2.5   On construct validity
321   User identity can be elicited in a number of ways. For example, Hofmann et al.
322   (2024) show that dialect alone is enough to make a model’s decisions covertly racist.
323   Lin et al. (2025) further apply this form of identity elicitation, showing that a model
324   prompted in African American Vernacular (AAVE) systematically performs worse
325   on mathematical reasoning benchmarks than when prompted in Standard American
326   English (SAE). Additionally, Kearney et al. (2025) find that conversational histories
327   from users with di!erent demographic profiles can alter the factual claims a model
328   makes.

329   Names, explicit labels, conversation history, location, dialect, all signal some form of
330   user identity, eliciting some change in model behaviour. It is tempting to treat them
331   as interchangeable ways of telling a model who a user is, but they are not. Tonneau
332   et al. (2026) find that cues meant to signal the same group (e.g. a Black-sounding
333   name vs. a prompt in AAVE) only partially overlap with changes in behaviour,
334   so that estimated disparities in LLM behaviour are unstable in both magnitude
335   and direction. They suggest that the instability may be due to variation in how
336   strongly a cue encodes the attribute, and linguistic confounders that shape behaviour
337   independently of it. Neplenbroek et al. (2026) highlight the former, showing that
338   models infer socio-demographics strongly from explicit mentions and stereotypical
339   cues, but only weakly from naturalistic conversation, the closet proxy for real-world
340   use.

341   This exemplifies the construct validity problem Bean et al. (2025) identify for
342   benchmarks. Simply put, a cue is not always a transparent proxy for the attribute
343   it signals. It also carries linguistic, cultural, and pragmatic context which means
344   that di!erent versions of the same attribute may in practice measure di!erent things.
345   Then, if estimated disparities are highly sensitive to how demographic constructs


                                                13
346   are operationalized, ecological validity becomes especially important (Tonneau et al.,
347   2026).

348   A measurement is said to be ecologically valid when it captures the phenomenon
349   under the conditions in which it actually occurs (Bean et al., 2025). For LLM political
350   influence, those conditions are open-ended writing assistance (Röttger et al., 2024),
351   delivered to a user the system holds beliefs about, particularly through the memory
352   mechanism that dominates what assistants retain about users (Dash et al., 2026)
353   and drives personalisation most strongly in real use (Jain et al., 2026).

354   2.6     Research questions
355   The preceding sections establish that models mirror politics a user states (2.2), act
356   on identity a user only implies (2.3), and that whether either constitutes stereotyping
357   can be judged only against a real distribution of group opinion (2.4).

358   Taking these together, we decompose the significance of memory-based political
359   personalisation in LLMs. This depends on three open empirical questions:

360         • To what extent does the presence and type of a user-identity memory cue shift
361           the political stance of writing-assistance outputs?

362         • Do model-induced stance shifts match demographic group di!erences observed
363           in the Cooperative Election Study (CES)?

364         • Do models represent the identity a cue signals, and does that representation
365           correspond to what they write?

368   As Figure 1 illustrates, the main experiment is a counterfactual prompting audit. This
369   design follows the tradition in discrimination research, in which identical materials
370   are submitted while varying only an identity signal, so that any systematic di!erence
371   in outcome can be attributed to the signal [cite].

372   The object of measurement is the political stance of a model’s response when asked
373   for open-ended writing assistance on a political issue, and the treatment is a single
374   cue about who the user appears to be, delivered as a stored user memory mimicking
375   the memory feature of ChatGPT and similar assistants.

376   Holding the issue and writing template fixed, we vary only the user cue and measure
377   the resulting stance. The bias elicited by each cue is then operationalised as the
378   distribution of stances in model responses across templates for a given issue. Because
379   every model response has a matched no-cue counterpart drawn from the same issue
380   → template pair, the design isolates the causal e!ect of the cue on the model’s output.
381   Therefore, any systematic di!erence in model stance to the same writing request is
382   attributable to the cue, not to the topic or the writing genre of the request.

384   Writing templates

385   The templates are drawn from the IssueBench (Röttger et al., 2025) template corpus,
386   which is built from real writing-assistance requests taken from three open source
387   conversation datasets (WildChat, LMSYS-Chat, ShareGPT) and reduced to reusable
388   templates by making the writing topic replaceable. Starting form their public 1,000
389   template sample, we filter to templates that [inclusion/exclusion criteria]. This leaves
390   145 templates for prompt construction.3

391   Issues

392   The Cooperative Election Study (CES) is a large, nationally representative US survey
393   conducted before and after each US presidential and midterm elections. It carries
394   partisanship, ideology, and opinions on policy issues from 17,000 US citizens. We
395   adopt the 2025 wave as the source bank for the political issues used as prompt topics.

398   Cues

399   We test four types of cue, each conveying personal information about the user, meant
400   to signal their political leaning at varying levels of directness. The first cue is drawn
401   from the three US party labels (Democrat, Republican, and Independent), telling
402   the model the user’s party a"liation (e.g. “The user is a Democrat”). The second
403   cue is drawn from the 50 US states, telling the user’s state of residence (e.g. “The
404   user is from California”). The third gives the race and gender (e.g. “The user is a
405   black woman”). The fourth provides the user’s first name (e.g. “The user’s name
406   is Terrell”), drawn from birth-record data for each race → gender group (Elder and
407   Hayes, 2023; Rosenman et al., 2023; Tzioumis, 2018).

408   The prompt

409   A single prompt is the composition of three independent parts as shown in Figure 2:
410   a cue (delivered as memory), a template (the writing genre and framing), an issue
411   (the topic inserted into the slot).

412   The cues are injected as an inferred user memory in the system prompt, following
413   the user-knowledge-memory format that widely-adopted LLM assistants use to carry
414   context across sessions (Mireshghallah et al., 2025). The baseline condition receives
415   no memory entry, so the comparison is between a model that holds stored information
416   about the user and the same model holding none.

430   All model responses are generated three times for test–retest reliability while keeping
431   inference costs manageable. IssueBench’s (Röttger et al., 2025) downsampling
432   analysis found that reducing from five generations to a single generation produced
433   negligible changes in aggregate evaluation outcomes, suggesting that only a small
434   number of repeated samples is su"cient for robust measurement. Our choice of
435   three generations therefore provides a compromise between estimating within-prompt
436   variability and computational e"ciency.

437   The explicit political and race → gender cues are a single reportable group membership
438   (e.g. a ‘Democrat’, or a ‘White man’), whereas the geographical and first name cues
439   are one realisation of a group (e.g. the name Brad is one of many White-male names,
440   Texas is one of many states). These two kinds cannot be sampled the same way
441   without conflating instance-level noise with group-level e!ect, so the design splits

453   3.4     Stance scoring
454   To measure a cue’s e!ect on the model’s response, each response is scored by a
455   fine-tuned stance classifier (DeBERTa-v3 cross-encoder), which outputs a continuous
456   stance score on a 0–100 scale. Scores are classified as: below 40 = conservative
457   leaning (↑1); 40-60 inclusive = neutral (0); above 60 = liberal leaning (+1).

458   We fine-tune this classifier on the AI distortion dataset (Röttger et al., 2026). These
459   are human/model edited paragraphs on a political proposition, each paired with
460   a human-annotated stance label on a 0–100 scale. There are 10,000 proposition
461   → paragraph examples across 100 propositions. This data set is well suited to the
462   measurement task because its labels are reader-aggregated judgments of writer stance
463   toward a political proposition, collected over AI-assisted and human-written political
464   opinion paragraphs, which is precisely the same construct, in the same genre, as
465   the responses scored in this thesis, with ground truth aggregated over roughly 2.9
466   million human ratings (Röttger et al., 2026).

467   For this task, a fine-tuned encoder is preferred over LLM-as-a-judge. It is deterministic
468   and reproducible; it is auditable against held-out human labels rather than itself an
469   unvalidated generative system, and it avoids the circularity of one instruction-tuned
470   LLM with political dispositions of its own adjudicating the politics of another.
